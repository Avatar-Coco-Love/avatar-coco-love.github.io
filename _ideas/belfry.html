Belfry

A puzzle game you can hear. Ring a set of church bells through every order they can make, one swap at a time, and bring them home without ever repeating a row.

  • Genre sound and logic puzzle
  • Audience puzzle fans, maths and music classrooms, bell ringers
  • Session 5–20 min
  • Size M (about a quarter of Foldwork)
  • Spreads by Daily Touch card, links that play a composition

The rules come from change ringing, an English tradition written down in 1668. Ringers still practise it in thousands of towers. I found practice simulators for ringers but no game built on these rules, and a 400-year-old tradition belongs to no one, so there is nothing to copy by accident.

Try the core rule

4 bells · 24 possible rows

Tap a gap between two bells to cross them, then ring. A bell moves one place at most, so two gaps side by side can't both cross. Tap a bell to draw its path in blue. Keys: 1–3 toggle gaps, Enter rings.

The idea in full

In a church tower each bell hangs on a wheel with one ringer below it. They ring in order, lightest to heaviest: 1234, called rounds. The lightest bell is the treble and the heaviest is the tenor. A bell that heavy can't be hurried or held back by more than one beat, so between one row and the next a bell can only swap with a neighbour. Ringers call each new row a change.

That gives three rules, and the whole game grows from them:

  1. Every row, every bell rings once.
  2. Between rows, a bell moves one place at most.
  3. No row twice, and finish on rounds. A composition that repeats a row is false. One that rings every possible row once is an extent.

Four bells make 24 rows, five make 120, six make 720 and eight make 40,320. Finding a path through them is a real search problem. Ringers have studied it since the 1600s, so the depth is already proven, and mathematicians teach it as a picture of permutation groups. The game adds goals, limits and a score to that search. The sound gives feedback a grid puzzle can't: a false row is one you have already heard.

Three ways to play

Each mode uses the same rows and the same rules, so a player can go from planning a composition to ringing it to leading the band through it.

Main mode

Compose

The puzzle. You see the current row and cross pairs of bells to make the next one, as in the demo above. Each tower sets a goal: reach a target row in exactly 7 changes, ring 18 different rows and come home, or avoid the cracked rows. Undo is free. After every change you hear the row, and you can play back the whole touch at any time.

Unlocks in tower 4

Ring

Rhythm. Your finished composition plays, the band rings their bells, and you ring one bell yourself with a tap or a key. You have to know where your bell is in each row, which is what a real ringer learns. You're judged on striking, the evenness of the beat, the way ringing competitions are judged. There's a no-timing option for anyone who wants it.

Unlocks in tower 5

Conduct

Live calls. The band rings a known pattern (Plain Bob) by itself. At each lead end you can call Bob, Single or nothing, and that changes what comes next. Your goal is to bring the band back to rounds without a false row. This is how real conductors work, and it's the deepest mode: the same three choices over and over, planned several leads ahead.

The picture is the solution

Ringers learn a pattern by drawing one bell's path through the rows. They usually draw it in blue with the treble in red, and they call it the blue line. In Belfry the line draws itself as you play. Every finished puzzle leaves a picture of how you solved it.

That gives the game three useful things:

  • A share image for the Daily card and the end of a tower.
  • A visual way in for players who can't hear, or have the sound off. The whole game works from the lines alone.
  • A clue for wasted moves. A dodge looks like a little spike. A smooth line usually means a smooth solution.

A composition also fits in a few characters. Ringers write each change as the places that don't move (x means every pair crosses), so a whole touch is a short string like x14x14x14x12. A link carrying that string opens the game and plays the touch. Nothing is stored on a server.

Tower rules

Each tower adds limits to the three rules. They're shown on the screen as plain icons and words, never by colour alone. Each one teaches one idea, then later towers combine them.

Towers: the progression

Eight towers, each a set of 8–12 puzzles, plus the Daily. Bigger towers mean more bells and more rows. The names are generic on purpose, so no real church or ringing society is shown.

Tower 8 isn't rung by hand. You compose it from blocks (whole leads of a method plus your calls), then listen to it rung at full speed, about three hours squeezed into a few minutes.

Scoring and peal boards

Each puzzle gives up to three bells:

  • True: no repeated row, finished on rounds, every tower rule kept.
  • Goal: the tower's target met (a length, a row, a time).
  • Craft: the puzzle's extra challenge, which is either more music or fewer changes than the reference solution.

Long achievements earn a peal board. Real towers record great rings on painted boards with gold lettering. In Belfry they hang on the wall of your ringing chamber, the game's home screen. That's the collection that keeps a player coming back past ten minutes: each board is a record of something hard you did.

The Daily Touch

One puzzle a day, the same for everyone, made from a seed by the generator described below and proven solvable before it ships. Monday starts on 4 bells; the week gets longer and harder until Sunday, which is on 6.

The result card doesn't give the answer away. It shows the length, the bells earned and a row of pips, one per change: · for a quiet change, ♪ for a music row. The full blue line picture unlocks for sharing the next day, once that puzzle has closed.

The Daily is the main way new players arrive, so it's in the first playable build, not the last.

A first session

Someone opens a shared Daily link on their phone with no idea what change ringing is.

  1. 0:00Three bells ring rounds. One line of text: "Swap two neighbours. Don't ring the same order twice." They tap a gap; the bells cross and ring the new order.
  2. 0:40They've rung all 6 rows of three bells. The blue line draws itself, and a peal board appears for the first "extent". It's tiny, but it's theirs.
  3. 2:00Four bells. A target row in 5 changes. Their first try comes in a change short; parity becomes something they feel, not something they read.
  4. 5:00First false row. The two matching rows flash and play one after the other, so they hear why it failed.
  5. 9:00They finish the Daily Touch with two bells out of three, then go back to find the shorter route for the third.
  6. 14:00They tap "Ring plain hunt" on the help card, hear an 8-row pattern ring itself, and realise it solves half of tower 2. That's the hook for the next session.

How puzzles are made and checked

Rows are small numbers, so the whole game state is tiny and fully checkable. On 4 to 6 bells a plain depth-first search finds every route quickly; on 8 bells puzzles are built from known method blocks instead.

  • Solver: proves every handmade puzzle and every Daily has a solution, and finds the shortest one (the Craft target).
  • Generator: picks a tower's rules from the seed, then keeps only puzzles the solver can solve and the novice bot can't solve by luck.
  • Bots: a novice (random legal crosses, avoids rows it has rung), a hunter (only uses plain hunt), and an expert (the solver). One line per bot per tower, the same style as the arcade's balance scripts.
  • CI: replays every reference solution after every change, as Foldwork does with its floor proofs.

Tech, phones and access

  • Sound: every bell is synthesized with Web Audio from the five partials of a tuned bell (hum, prime, tierce, quint, nominal), so there are no audio files. The demo above uses the same method. Strikes are scheduled on the audio clock, so timing holds on slow phones.
  • Drawing: Canvas 2D for the bells and lines; the system font stack in the game itself.
  • Input: tap or click a gap, or number keys on PC. Every target is 44px or larger.
  • Seeded: every Daily and every generated puzzle comes from a seed.
  • Hearing: every strike also flashes, and Compose is fully playable with sound off.
  • Colour: bells carry numbers, so lines and bells never rely on red and blue alone.
  • Ring mode timing: a calibration tap test for Bluetooth headphone lag, adjustable timing windows, and a no-timing option.
  • Offline and private: saves stay on the device; play data only if the player opts in.

Arcade game or standalone?

Recommendation: build Belfry as a standalone game, like Foldwork, and list it on the projects page.

  • Links that play a composition and the Daily card work best when the link opens the game itself, not a cabinet inside the gallery's sandboxed frame.
  • Three modes, eight towers and a home screen of peal boards are bigger than one self-contained arcade file.
  • The arcade roadmap favours depth on its 20 games while player counts are low, so a new arcade game would go against it.

The arcade could still get a taste later: a single-tower cabinet (4 bells, Compose only, a score, three achievements) that links to the full game. That's optional and not part of this plan.

A first build plan

One PR each. A phone-playable prototype is 4 PRs away (highlighted). The full game is about 11 PRs.

  1. Scaffolding. Repository belfry, page, test runner, CI, offline shell, CLAUDE.md and docs written from this page. Reuse Foldwork's seeded random generator, storage wrapper and changelog pattern.
  2. Rows core. Rows, changes, place notation, truth checking, music detection, the solver. Pure logic, no page access, unit tested.
  3. Bell sound. Synthesized bells for 3–8 bells, a strike scheduler with the handstroke gap, a mute switch. A test page to listen to each bell.
  4. Compose screen. Bells, gaps, undo, row list, blue line, towers 1–2. Playable prototype: try it on a phone here.
  5. Puzzle engine. Tower rules, three bells per puzzle, reference solutions replayed in CI, tower 3, and the balance script with its bots.
  6. Daily Touch. Seeded generator with solver proof, the result card, links that play a composition.
  7. Ring mode. Striking judge, calibration, timing options; tower 4.
  8. Conduct mode. Plain Bob, bobs and singles, lead ends; tower 5.
  9. Ringing chamber. Home screen and peal boards.
  10. Towers 6–8. Music, 6 and 8 bells, composing from method blocks, fast playback.
  11. Finish. Settings, reduced motion, privacy page, share image, projects page card.

Risks

  • An obscure theme. "Church bells" can sound dusty. The pitch leads with the puzzle and the sound; the history is flavour, never homework.
  • Small boards early on. Four bells only have 24 rows, so towers 2–3 could run out of surprises. Tower rules and the Craft goal carry them; playtests decide if 5 bells should come sooner.
  • Timing on phones. Ring mode depends on low input lag. Audio-clock scheduling and calibration should cover it; test on a real phone right after PR 7.
  • Getting the tradition right. Ringers will notice wrong terms. Ask a real ringer to read the glossary and Conduct mode before launch.
  • The name. "Belfry" is a common word and other projects may use it. Check before the repository is made.
