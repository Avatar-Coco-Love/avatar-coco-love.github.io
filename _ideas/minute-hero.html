Idea D · top-down adventure · concept

Minute Hero

A small adventure played one minute at a time. A curse gives you sixty seconds per life. Each life you get a little further, and whatever you fix stays fixed.

  • 60 s lives
  • ~40 screens
  • 8 tools
  • 12 Hours to bring home
  • phones and PCs

The idea in full

Pip is the apprentice bell-ringer of Tickhollow, a valley that keeps time by the great bell in its clock tower. One night the Magpie steals the bell's clapper, and the valley's hours go with it. Since then Tickhollow lives one minute at a time. Every sixty seconds the valley rewinds to the same morning minute and everyone forgets. Pip is the only one who remembers.

When the minute runs out, Pip wakes in the Still House, the bell-ringer's cottage. Its grandfather clock stopped at 11:59, and the curse can't get in. There is no timer at home. You look at the map, read your notes, pet the cat, and step out of the door when you're ready.

The world remembers what Pip changes. A gate you unbar stays open, a bridge you lower stays down, and a key in your pocket stays there. So each life is a short trip with one job: dig up the key in the dunes, light the sundial at the shore, find out where the ferry goes. Running out of time costs you nothing but your place on the map.

The goal is to bring back the twelve Hours the Magpie scattered across the valley, mend the bell, and ring it to call the Magpie down from the Roost at the top of the tower.

What it adds to Minit

The idea comes from Minit (2018): a cursed sword, sixty-second lives and a tiny black-and-white world. Minute Hero keeps that core and adds three things.

  • The minute has a schedule. The valley replays the same sixty seconds every life, so things happen at set times: the ferry always leaves at 0:35. Learning the schedule is part of the puzzle, a small version of Majora's Mask.
  • A calm home with a memory. The Still House has no clock, a map you can pin notes on, and the trail of your last three lives.
  • Color, hats and gentler settings. A warm palette, fourteen hats to find, and a longer-minute option for young players.

Playing a life

The sixty-second rule

Every thing in the game is in one of three groups, and the player can always tell which.

Kept

In your pockets, forever.

  • tools
  • keys
  • the Hours
  • heart pieces
  • hats
  • notes and schedule lines
Fixed

Changes to the world that stay.

  • opened gates and doors
  • lowered bridges and ladders
  • lit sundials and lantern posts
  • dug tunnels
  • broken walls
Reset

The minute's clockwork, back each life.

  • enemies
  • pushed crates
  • switches and timed doors
  • your hearts
  • things you carry (a pie, a bucket)
  • where people stand

The rule for building it: if the player worked for it, it stays. If it belongs to the minute, it resets. Losing all your hearts ends the life early, which is the same as running out of time, just sooner.

Sundials

Each region has a sundial. Light it once and you can start any later life there. This is how the world reaches past a sixty-second walk from home: the far edge of the map is a minute from the nearest sundial, not from the Still House.

Each Hour sits at the end of a short dungeon or a hard secret. Dungeons are small on purpose: once you know the way and have opened its shortcuts, one fits in a minute. The first time through takes several lives, and each life opens one more door inside it.

Tools

Each tool opens new places and also makes old routes faster, so the same minute reaches further as the game goes on.

Other things you keep

    Home: the Still House

    How a session feels

    A nine-minute bus ride, a few hours into the game. Each bar is one life, sixty seconds wide. The shaded part is how long it lasted, and the brass mark is the moment that mattered.

    0:000:300:60

      Six lives, about nine minutes: a key, a sundial, a schedule line, a heart piece and a hat. Every minute ended with something.

      The first ten minutes

      Life one has no warning. Pip wakes, wanders the square, and at the minute collapses and wakes in bed again. The watch ring appears on the second life, once you've seen it happen. By life four you have the Twig Sword from the market; by life seven you have cut into Pocketwood and lit its sundial; around life nine the first Hour comes up out of the well. A new player should understand the minute, the schedule and sundials inside ten minutes.

      Keeping the clock calm

      The timer is the hook and also the biggest risk: it can make the game feel stressful. Everything here is meant to make the minute feel like a measuring stick.

      • Home has no clock. Menus, the map and dialogue all stop the timer, so reading speed never costs time.
      • The last ten seconds tick softly and the watch ring turns warm orange. No alarm, no red flash.
      • The end of a life is a chime and a summary card, never the words "game over".
      • Every goal has a route of 45 seconds or less, checked by a test bot, so a good route always leaves slack.
      • After five lives in a row with nothing new, the cat offers a hint. You can ignore it.
      • An assist setting gives longer minutes (75, 90 or 120 seconds) for young players. Daily results show which setting was used.
      • With reduced motion on, the end-of-life sweep becomes a plain fade.

      How it spreads

      The Daily Minute. Once a day everyone gets the same tiny seeded map and one life to collect as many coins as they can. The result card draws your route as a line, so two friends can compare their paths at a glance.

      Lives as the score. Finishing the game says "Finished in 143 lives". A low number is a speedrun goal anyone can understand.

      Clip-sized. A whole life fits in a sixty-second video.

      Controls

      Phone: a floating joystick under the left thumb. On the right, a big sword button and a smaller tool button; tap the tool icon to swap tools. The boots wind up while you hold the tool button and dash when you let go.

      PC: arrow keys or WASD to move, J or Z for the sword, K or X for the tool, Tab to swap, M for the map. A game controller works too.

      Look: 16 × 16 pixel tiles and one fixed palette, all drawn in code, every sound synthesized, the same rules as Foldwork. The mockups on this page are drawn that way.

      Size and a first slice

      The smallest of the six adventure ideas: about 40 screens, close to Minit's size. It builds on the "start small, built to grow" foundations in the ideas file and adds two pieces:

      • The schedule as data: events keyed to seconds, so timings live in one table.
      • A timed reachability check: a bot finds the fastest route to every goal from each sundial, using only the tools you could have by then, and fails if any goal needs more than 45 seconds. When a timing changes, this catches the puzzles it breaks.
      1. Engine, the minute timer, the Still House and saves.
      2. Sword, hearts, beetles and the end-of-life card.
      3. Tickhollow and Pocketwood (about 12 screens), the baker and bridge events, the first sundial.
      4. Clockwork Boots, the Well dungeon and the first Hour.
      5. Map wall, notebook, the timed reachability check, sound, and a phone playtest.

      After those five PRs: about 20 to 30 minutes of play (around 25 lives) and 2 of the 12 Hours. The rest is mostly new data.

      Risks

      • Brittle timing. Moving one event by two seconds can break a puzzle elsewhere. The timed check exists for this.
      • Repetition. Walking the same first ten seconds every life gets dull. Sundials, dug tunnels and the mine cart are there to cut it.
      • Dash precision on phones. The boots need a forgiving aim (snap to eight directions) and a playtest early.
      • Too close to Minit. The schedule, the hub and the color are what keep it its own game; they should all be in the first slice.

      Open questions for you

      1. The hero. Pip the bell-ringer's apprentice, or someone else (a fox, a mouse, a robot)?
      2. Dialogue stops the timer. I recommend yes. Minit didn't, and it punished slow readers.
      3. The Echo Bell. In the final dungeon, or saved for later? It's the most complex item to build and test.
      4. Sprites. Stored as text in the code (the Foldwork rule) or as small image files?
      5. Where it lives. Standalone like Foldwork is my pick: an adventure doesn't fit one arcade file. The Daily Minute alone could become an arcade game later.
